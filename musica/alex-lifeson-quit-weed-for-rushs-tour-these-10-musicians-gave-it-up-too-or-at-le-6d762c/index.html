Alex Lifeson Quit Weed for Rush’s Tour. These 10 Musicians Gave It Up, Too (Or At Least Tried)

What's happening

Alex Lifeson, guitarist for the Canadian rock band Rush, quit cannabis use ahead of the band's tour. Rolling Stone has compiled a list of ten other musicians—including Miley Cyrus, Willie Nelson, Paul McCartney, and SZA—who have similarly quit or attempted to quit using marijuana.

Who's involved

Alex Lifeson is the guitarist and co-founder of Rush, the legendary Canadian rock band formed in Toronto in 1968. Rush maintained its classic three-member lineup of Lifeson, Geddy Lee, and Neil Peart from 1974 until the band's initial dissolution in 2015, eventually reforming in 2025.

Why it matters

Lifeson's decision highlights how even veteran musicians at the peak of their careers reassess their personal habits in preparation for major performances. The broader list of musicians who have quit or reduced cannabis use reveals shifting attitudes toward substance use within the music industry, driven by health concerns, creative clarity, and professional demands.

The story

Alex Lifeson has made the decision to quit marijuana as Rush prepares for its upcoming tour, joining a notable group of musicians who have taken similar steps. The guitarist and co-founder of Rush, one of rock's most enduring acts, is prioritizing sobriety ahead of what will be a significant moment for the band that has commanded devoted audiences since its formation in Toronto in 1968.

Lifeson's choice is far from unique in the music world. According to Rolling Stone, a range of high-profile artists have publicly discussed quitting cannabis or attempting to do so, including Miley Cyrus, Willie Nelson, Paul McCartney, and SZA. The magazine compiled a list of ten musicians with similar stories, underscoring that this decision crosses genre lines and career stages.

Rush itself carries particular weight in rock history. The band's classic lineup—Lifeson on guitar alongside Geddy Lee on bass and vocals and Neil Peart on drums—took shape in 1974 and remained unchanged for more than four decades until the band's initial dissolution in 2015. That stability and longevity made Rush a powerhouse in progressive rock, known for technical virtuosity and elaborate compositions that demanded peak cognitive performance from all members.

For touring musicians, the physical and mental demands are substantial. Extended travel, nightly performances, and the need for precision in complex arrangements all benefit from clarity and focus. Lifeson's decision to eliminate cannabis use before hitting the road reflects a practical reassessment of what his body and mind require to deliver the performances that Rush's audience expects.

The willingness of musicians across different genres and generations to discuss their substance use—whether quitting, reducing, or maintaining—marks a shift in how the industry talks about these issues. Where previous decades saw drug use largely treated as part of rock mythology, contemporary artists are more candid about making deliberate health choices. This transparency may encourage fans and younger musicians to examine their own habits without shame.

As Lifeson and Rush prepare for their tour, the guitarist's decision serves as a reminder that even veteran performers continuously reassess their personal practices in service of their craft.
