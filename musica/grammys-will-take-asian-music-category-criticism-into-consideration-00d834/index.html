Grammys Will Take Asian Music Category Criticism Into Consideration

What's happening

The Recording Academy is reconsidering its approach to the Best Asian Pop Music Performance Grammy category after facing criticism when BTS decided not to submit their work. CEO Harvey Mason Jr. initially defended the category but is now open to feedback from industry stakeholders about potential changes.

Who's involved

Harvey Mason Jr., CEO of the Recording Academy, which oversees the Grammy Awards—the major U.S. music awards recognizing outstanding achievements across genres. BTS, the seven-member South Korean boy band formed in 2010, whose decision to opt out of submission triggered the broader conversation about the category's structure.

Why it matters

The Grammy Awards carry significant prestige and industry influence, making categorical decisions consequential for how music is recognized and valued globally. How the Academy addresses concerns about the Asian Pop Music category could signal whether major awards bodies are responsive to artist feedback and willing to evolve their recognition frameworks.

The story

The Grammy Awards' approach to recognizing Asian music is facing fresh scrutiny after BTS opted out of submitting to the Best Asian Pop Music Performance category, prompting the Recording Academy to reconsider its stance. CEO Harvey Mason Jr. initially defended the category when the band's decision became public, but according to Rolling Stone, new conversations with stakeholders may lead the Academy to take the criticism more seriously and explore potential modifications.

BTS, the seven-member South Korean boy band that has dominated global charts since their 2010 formation, wields considerable influence in the music industry. The group consists of Jin, Suga, J-Hope, RM, Jimin, V, and Jung Kook, who co-write or co-produce much of their material. Originally performing as a hip hop group, they have since expanded their musical palette to encompass a wide range of genres, with lyrics addressing themes including mental health, coming-of-age struggles, and self-love. Their decision to bypass the Asian Pop Music category suggests dissatisfaction with how the category is structured or positioned within the broader Grammy framework.

The question of how major awards recognize music from different regions and cultures remains contentious within the industry. Some observers have questioned whether segregating Asian artists into a separate category, rather than allowing them to compete in mainstream pop categories, inadvertently limits their visibility and prestige. Others have raised concerns about the scope of the category itself—whether it adequately represents the diversity of musical styles and national origins across Asia, or whether it creates a catch-all that dilutes meaningful recognition.

Harvey Mason Jr.'s initial defense of the category suggested the Academy viewed it as an inclusive expansion of Grammy recognition. However, his willingness to engage in new conversations with stakeholders signals potential openness to revising that position. The Recording Academy's responsiveness to feedback from major artists like BTS could reshape how future Grammy categories are designed, particularly as the global music industry continues to shift and artists from outside the United States command larger shares of streaming revenue and chart success.

The outcome of these conversations may carry implications beyond the Asian Pop Music category. How the Academy resolves this discussion could influence broader conversations about whether specialized categories genuinely honor artistic achievement or inadvertently create hierarchies within the awards themselves. For BTS and other internationally successful artists, the decision whether to engage with Grammy categories going forward may hinge on whether the Academy demonstrates meaningful change.
