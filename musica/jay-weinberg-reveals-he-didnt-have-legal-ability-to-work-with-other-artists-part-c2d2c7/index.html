Jay Weinberg Reveals He Didn’t Have ‘Legal Ability’ to Work With Other Artists: Part of Him ‘Was Dying’

What's happening

Jay Weinberg, drummer for Slipknot, has revealed that contractual restrictions prevented him from working with other artists, a limitation he describes as emotionally taxing. The musician is now publicly discussing how the lack of legal freedom to collaborate affected him personally and creatively.

Who's involved

Jay Weinberg is the drummer for Slipknot, the heavy metal band he joined in 2014 after replacing founding drummer Joey Jordison. Weinberg, son of Bruce Springsteen's longtime drummer Max Weinberg, has a history of playing with multiple acts including Against Me! and the E Street Band before his tenure with Slipknot.

Why it matters

Weinberg's comments shed light on the restrictive contractual terms major rock bands impose on members, which can limit artistic growth and personal fulfillment. His willingness to speak publicly about the emotional toll of such restrictions may prompt broader conversations within the music industry about artist autonomy and the balance between band exclusivity and individual creative freedom.

The story

Jay Weinberg has opened up about the professional constraints that shaped his time as Slipknot's drummer, revealing that contractual limitations prevented him from pursuing collaborative projects outside the band. According to Rolling Stone, the musician explained that "without giving too much attention to any kind of one situation, not having the legal ability to play music with other people was definitely difficult." The restriction, he indicated, took an emotional toll, with part of him feeling like it "was dying" during those periods.

Weinberg joined Slipknot in 2014, taking over the drum chair from founding member Joey Jordison. Before joining the Iowa-based metal act, Weinberg had established himself as a versatile drummer, having played with the punk rock band the Reveling and touring with Bruce Springsteen's E Street Band in 2009 as a substitute for his father, Max Weinberg, who has been Springsteen's drummer for decades. He also had brief stints with Madball and played with Against Me! during 2011 and 2012.

The contractual restrictions Weinberg describes are not uncommon in the music industry, particularly within established bands where members are expected to maintain exclusivity. However, such arrangements can create significant creative frustration for musicians who wish to explore side projects or collaborate with peers. Weinberg's candid remarks suggest that the emotional and artistic cost of such limitations extends beyond simple career management—it affects the psychological well-being of artists bound by them.

By publicly discussing these constraints, Weinberg joins a growing conversation among musicians about the need for greater flexibility in band contracts. His willingness to speak about the experience—and the personal toll it exacted—highlights the tension between an artist's desire for creative exploration and the business interests that drive major touring acts to demand exclusive commitment from their members.
