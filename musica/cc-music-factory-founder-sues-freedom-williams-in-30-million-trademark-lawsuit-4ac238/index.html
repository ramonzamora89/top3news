C+C Music Factory Founder Sues Freedom Williams in $30 Million Trademark Lawsuit

What's happening

Robert Clivillés, co-founder of C+C Music Factory, is suing rapper Freedom Williams for $30 million over trademark rights to the group's name. Clivillés claims Williams has been profiting from the C+C Music Factory brand without authorization.

Who's involved

Robert Clivillés co-founded C+C Music Factory in 1989 alongside David Cole; the group became a major act in late 1980s and early 1990s dance and hip-hop music. Freedom Williams is the original lead rapper of the group's biggest hits and currently holds the trademark rights to the C+C Music Factory name, which he acquired in 2003.

Why it matters

The lawsuit highlights ongoing disputes over intellectual property and band identity in the music industry, particularly when original members disagree on who controls a group's legacy and commercial rights. The outcome could set precedent for how trademark ownership is determined among founding members of long-defunct musical acts.

The story

Robert Clivillés, the co-founder of the 1990s dance and hip-hop group C+C Music Factory, has filed a $30 million trademark lawsuit against rapper Freedom Williams, alleging that Williams has been using and profiting from the group's name without permission. According to Rolling Stone, Clivillés claims that Williams has made substantial income from touring and other commercial activities under the C+C Music Factory banner.

C+C Music Factory was established in 1989 by Clivillés and David Cole and rose to prominence with a string of hit singles including "Gonna Make You Sweat," "Here We Go," "Things That Make You Go Hmmm...," "Just a Touch of Love," and "Keep It Comin'." Williams, who served as the group's lead rapper on these tracks, became closely associated with the act's commercial success. The band ceased recording following Cole's death in 1996, though the group later reformed in 2010 with Eric Kupper stepping in to replace Cole.

The crux of the dispute centers on trademark ownership. Williams acquired the legal rights to the C+C Music Factory name in 2003 and has continued to tour and perform under that banner. Clivillés's lawsuit contests Williams's exclusive right to use the name, arguing that the original co-founder and his estate should retain ownership or at least control over how the brand is commercialized.

The case underscores longstanding tensions within the music industry over artistic identity and intellectual property when founding members of successful groups pursue divergent paths. While Williams has the registered trademark, Clivillés's legal challenge suggests he believes the co-founder's original creative contribution entitles him to a stake in the group's commercial exploitation. The $30 million figure reflects the perceived value of the C+C Music Factory brand and its enduring appeal to audiences decades after the group's original run.
