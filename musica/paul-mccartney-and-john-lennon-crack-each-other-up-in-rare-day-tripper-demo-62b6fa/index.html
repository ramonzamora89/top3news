Paul McCartney and John Lennon Crack Each Other Up in Rare ‘Day Tripper’ Demo

What's happening

A rare demo recording of the Beatles' "Day Tripper" is surfacing, capturing Paul McCartney and John Lennon joking and laughing together during a songwriting session at Lennon's house. The "Songwriting Work Tape" version will be included on an upcoming special edition of Rubber Soul.

Who's involved

Paul McCartney and John Lennon, the songwriting partnership behind the Beatles, one of the most influential rock bands in history. "Day Tripper" was primarily written by Lennon with contributions from McCartney and released as a double A-side single with "We Can Work It Out" in December 1965.

Why it matters

Unreleased demos and work tapes from the Beatles' recording sessions are rare archival finds that offer fans and historians intimate glimpses into the creative process of one of music's greatest songwriting duos. The release underscores the enduring commercial and cultural value of Beatles material, with new editions continuing to drive interest in their catalog decades after the band's breakup.

The story

A candid demo recording of "Day Tripper" is being released for the first time, capturing an unguarded moment between Paul McCartney and John Lennon as they worked through the classic song. According to Rolling Stone, the "Songwriting Work Tape" version was recorded at Lennon's house and will appear on an upcoming special edition of Rubber Soul, the Beatles' sixth studio album from 1965. The tape preserves the two musicians laughing and cracking each other up, offering a rare window into their creative collaboration in the studio.

"Day Tripper" holds an important place in Beatles history. Released in December 1965 as a double A-side single paired with "We Can Work It Out," the track was primarily written by John Lennon with some contributions from Paul McCartney, credited to the Lennon–McCartney partnership. Both songs were recorded during the sessions for Rubber Soul, which was released on 3 December 1965 on EMI's Parlophone label in the United Kingdom. The single proved to be a commercial success, topping the charts in Britain, Ireland, the Netherlands, and Norway.

The emergence of this demo recording is significant for Beatles archivists and fans alike. Work tapes and informal studio recordings from the band's early years are exceedingly rare, as much of their recorded output from the 1960s was either officially released or has remained in private vaults. This particular tape is notable not only for its historical value but for the intimate quality it captures—the easy rapport between Lennon and McCartney as they composed, with moments of levity punctuating their creative process. Such artifacts provide context that finished recordings cannot offer, revealing the spontaneity and humor that characterized their songwriting sessions.

Rubber Soul itself was described as an important artistic achievement for the band upon its release, and it remains a landmark album in rock music history. The album's special edition will bring together previously unreleased material alongside the original recordings, appealing to both longtime collectors and new listeners discovering the Beatles' catalog. The inclusion of the "Day Tripper" work tape suggests a broader effort to document and preserve the band's creative legacy in ways that celebrate both the finished product and the human moments behind it.
