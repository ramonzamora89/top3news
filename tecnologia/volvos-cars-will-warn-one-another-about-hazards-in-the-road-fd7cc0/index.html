Volvo’s cars will warn one another about hazards in the road

What's happening

Volvo is rolling out new connected safety features across three of its electric vehicles that allow cars to communicate directly with one another and alert drivers about hazards ahead, including animals and vulnerable road users. This represents a shift from crowdsourced alert systems toward vehicle-to-vehicle communication.

Who's involved

Volvo Cars, a Swedish multinational luxury vehicle manufacturer headquartered in Gothenburg, is leading this initiative. The company has long centered its marketing around safety innovation and Swedish design heritage, making this advancement consistent with its core brand positioning.

Why it matters

Vehicle-to-vehicle hazard warnings could significantly reduce accidents by enabling real-time, direct communication between cars about roadside dangers before human drivers spot them. Over time, this type of connected safety infrastructure may become a critical competitive differentiator in the luxury vehicle market and a model for broader industry adoption.

The story

Volvo Cars is equipping three of its electric vehicles with an updated suite of connected safety features designed to help drivers avoid collisions with animals and vulnerable road users. The new system leverages direct communication between vehicles rather than relying on crowdsourced data platforms, creating a more immediate warning network for hazards encountered on the road.

The Swedish luxury automaker has built its reputation on safety innovation and premium design since its founding, and this latest development reinforces that positioning. According to The Verge, the new hazard alerts function by having Volvo cars relay real-time information about obstacles and dangers to other vehicles in the network, enabling drivers to receive warnings well before they might visually detect a threat themselves.

This vehicle-to-vehicle communication approach differs fundamentally from existing crowdsourced systems, where drivers or navigation apps manually report hazards after encountering them. By automating the warning process through direct car-to-car data sharing, Volvo's system potentially offers faster, more reliable alert transmission. The technology targets scenarios where reaction time is critical—sudden animal crossings, pedestrians, cyclists, and other unpredictable road users that traditional driver alertness might miss.

The three electric vehicles receiving this upgrade represent Volvo's core lineup in the EV segment, signaling the company's commitment to embedding connected safety features across its electrified portfolio. As Volvo continues expanding its electric vehicle manufacturing, these safety enhancements serve as both a practical protective tool and a marketing differentiator in the growing luxury EV market.

While crowdsourced alert systems have value, they depend on user participation and contain inherent delays. By contrast, vehicle-to-vehicle communication happens instantaneously and automatically, reducing the human element that can compromise alert accuracy or timeliness. This represents a meaningful step toward autonomous safety systems that work independently of driver input.
