New York governor to 3D-printed gun leader: ‘tell Cody I will get ahead of him’

What's happening

Cody Wilson, creator of the first 3D-printed gun, has announced a new tool designed to circumvent New York's restrictions on 3D-printed firearms—and deliberately taunted Governor Kathy Hochul in the process. Hochul has fired back, vowing to outmaneuver him.

Who's involved

Cody Wilson is an American gun rights activist and crypto-anarchist who founded Defense Distributed, a non-profit that develops and publishes open-source gun designs. Governor Kathy Hochul, New York's first female governor since 2021, has positioned herself as an opponent of gun proliferation and 3D-printing technology.

Why it matters

This escalating clash highlights the ongoing tension between digital manufacturing technology and gun regulation in America. The conflict illustrates how determined activists can use open-source design and online distribution to challenge state-level firearms laws, while also demonstrating political willingness to confront such efforts directly.

The story

Cody Wilson, the gun rights activist behind Defense Distributed, has thrust himself back into the national spotlight with an announcement that directly challenges New York State's efforts to restrict 3D-printed firearms. Wilson published details of a new tool last week that he claims can help circumvent a New York law preventing 3D printers from manufacturing guns. In a pointed move, he accompanied the announcement with a taunt directed at Governor Kathy Hochul, seemingly daring her to respond.

Hochul accepted the challenge. Rather than letting the provocation pass, the New York governor issued a direct counter-message, declaring that she would move to get ahead of Wilson and his efforts. The exchange represents a rare moment of open, name-called confrontation between a state executive and a digital-age activist over the future of firearms manufacturing.

Wilson has a long track record with this issue. In 2013, his organization Defense Distributed gained international attention when it published online plans for the Liberator, the first widely available functioning 3D-printed pistol. That release sparked years of legal battles and public debate over whether open-source gun designs constitute protected speech or dangerous weapons manufacturing. According to The Verge, Wilson's latest move appears designed to test the boundaries of New York's newer safeguards against 3D-printed gun production.

Hochul's response signals that New York State intends to remain aggressive in defending its regulations. As the first female governor of New York and a Democrat, Hochul has positioned herself prominently on gun control issues. Her public commitment to "get ahead of" Wilson suggests she may be considering additional legislative or enforcement measures to close loopholes or accelerate regulation of the tools and techniques Wilson is promoting.

The standoff reflects a fundamental clash between technological accessibility and governmental authority. Wilson's crypto-anarchist philosophy emphasizes the democratization of manufacturing—the idea that digital designs and open-source sharing undermine centralized control. Hochul's position represents the opposing view: that government has both the right and responsibility to restrict dangerous technologies, particularly when they circumvent existing weapons laws.

What remains unclear is whether this public sparring will lead to concrete new legislation, enforcement actions, or simply remain a battle of rhetoric and competing visions for the future of manufacturing and individual liberty.
