An okay laptop with 16GB of RAM is better than a nice laptop with 8GB, and this $520 HP OmniBook proves it

What's happening

The Verge reviews HP's OmniBook 3, a $520 laptop that prioritizes practical specs like 16GB of RAM over premium build quality, challenging the notion that price alone determines laptop value. The review argues that adequate RAM matters more than cosmetic appeal when budgets are tight.

Who's involved

HP Inc., the world's second-largest PC vendor by unit sales, has revived its OmniBook line for consumer laptops featuring AI technology since 2024. The Verge, a major technology publication, is testing and evaluating the budget-friendly model.

Why it matters

As laptop prices have climbed without proportional performance gains, budget models with smart spec allocation—like base RAM of 16GB rather than 8GB—offer practical value that challenges premium pricing. This shift could influence how consumers and manufacturers think about PC configuration priorities in an increasingly expensive market.

The story

The laptop market has fundamentally shifted over the past decade, with price-to-performance ratios becoming increasingly skewed. What once cost $500 and provided a tolerable computing experience now often requires $700 or more, while the $900 sweet spot for genuinely capable machines has largely disappeared. According to The Verge, HP's new OmniBook 3 attempts to buck this trend by focusing on what actually matters for daily computing rather than premium materials or design flourishes.

HP Inc., the American multinational headquartered in Palo Alto and the world's second-largest PC manufacturer by unit sales, has strategically reintroduced the OmniBook brand for the consumer market. Originally launched in 1993, the OmniBook name was used for business laptops through 2002 before lying dormant; its return in 2024 marks a pivot toward consumer-focused machines emphasizing AI capabilities and practical affordability. The OmniBook 3 exemplifies this philosophy: priced at $520, it opts for functional specs over flashy construction.

The core argument embedded in this laptop's design is straightforward but increasingly countercultural: 16GB of RAM matters more than aluminum chassis or premium finishes. In an era where many mainstream laptops skimp on base memory to keep prices down, requiring buyers to pay extra for adequate multitasking capability, the OmniBook 3's standard configuration delivers genuine breathing room for applications and browser tabs. This decision reflects a recalibration of what budget-conscious buyers actually need to be productive.

The OmniBook 3 won't win admirers seeking a prestige device with an impressive unboxing experience. Its build quality sits comfortably in the "adequate" category—functional without inspiring particular enthusiasm. But this restraint in pursuing luxury enables the manufacturer to allocate resources where they yield tangible user benefit. As premium laptops have grown increasingly expensive while padding their specs minimally, a no-frills machine that nails the fundamentals represents a quiet rebellion against the bloated midmarket.

The broader implication is that laptop design priorities have become misaligned with actual buyer needs. Consumers often pay premium prices for materials and aesthetics while accepting compromised RAM and storage that slow daily work. The OmniBook 3 reverses this equation, betting that users would rather have a plain machine that performs smoothly than a pretty one that struggles. If this thesis proves correct, it could force the industry to rethink where value actually resides in personal computing.
