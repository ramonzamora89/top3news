Switched on Pop’s Nate Sloan and Charlie Harding love fresh vegetables and guitar pedals

What's happening

Nate Sloan and Charlie Harding, hosts of the acclaimed music podcast Switched on Pop, are profiled by The Verge in a feature that explores their personalities and interests beyond their deep dives into pop music analysis.

Who's involved

Nate Sloan is a musicologist and Charlie Harding is a songwriter; together they created Switched on Pop in 2014, a podcast dedicated to analyzing popular music that has become recognized as one of the best music podcasts available.

Why it matters

The profile offers insight into the creative minds behind one of contemporary music criticism's most influential platforms, humanizing the analytical voices that have shaped how millions of listeners understand pop music. Their documented interests—from fresh vegetables to guitar pedals—reveal the eclectic range of influences that inform their approach to musical criticism.

The story

Nate Sloan and Charlie Harding have built a reputation as the Lennon–McCartney of pop music analysis through their podcast Switched on Pop, which launched in 2014. According to The Verge, the duo brings an unusually deep analytical lens to contemporary hits, breaking down production, harmony, and cultural resonance in ways that have resonated with music fans seeking substance alongside streaming playlists.

The podcast's origin story traces back to Carly Rae Jepsen's "Call Me Maybe," which according to lore served as the genesis for the show. Sloan, a musicologist, and Harding, a songwriter, found in that deceptively simple earworm a perfect case study for what makes pop music tick. Rather than dismiss the track as frivolous, they recognized its architectural sophistication and cultural moment, setting the tone for the show's entire approach: taking pop seriously as a legitimate subject for rigorous analysis.

Beyond their podcast work, the profile reveals Sloan and Harding to be thoughtful individuals with surprisingly varied interests. Their passion for fresh vegetables and guitar pedals suggests a curiosity that extends well beyond music theory and production techniques. These seemingly disparate interests—horticultural freshness and sonic equipment—hint at a shared sensibility: a drive to understand how things work, whether in a kitchen, a studio, or on a record.

Since its 2014 debut, Switched on Pop has become a cultural touchstone for music criticism in the streaming era. The show's success lies partly in Sloan and Harding's genuine enthusiasm for their subject matter, refusing the pretension that often accompanies music analysis while maintaining intellectual rigor. They treat pop music not as a guilty pleasure or a commercial product to be disdained, but as worthy of the same close attention classical musicians and serious critics have long reserved for other genres.

The Verge's profile underscores how Sloan and Harding have helped legitimize pop music criticism during an era when streaming algorithms and social media have fragmented listening habits. Their work demonstrates that pop analysis need not be either condescending toward its subject or entirely uncritical in its approach. Instead, by combining Sloan's musicological training with Harding's insider knowledge of songwriting, they've created a framework for understanding contemporary music that appeals to casual listeners and music professionals alike.
