Sony Music and Warner Chappell are suing Anthropic

What's happening

Sony Music and Warner Chappell have filed a lawsuit against Anthropic in federal court, alleging the AI company used tens of thousands of copyrighted musical works without permission to train its Claude language model. The plaintiffs are seeking damages of up to $150,000 per work, potentially reaching into the billions.

Who's involved

Sony Music Entertainment is the recording division of Sony Group Corporation and one of the world's largest music companies. Warner Chappell is a major music publishing entity. Anthropic, PBC is a San Francisco-based AI company founded in 2021 by former OpenAI members including CEO Dario Amodei and President Daniela Amodei, best known for developing Claude, a series of large language models.

Why it matters

This case represents a significant legal challenge to how AI companies obtain training data, potentially setting precedent for the music industry's relationship with generative AI developers. The outcome could force major changes in how AI firms source and compensate for copyrighted content, with ripple effects across the broader AI training ecosystem.

The story

Sony Music and Warner Chappell have brought legal action against Anthropic in the US District Court for the Northern District of California, accusing the artificial intelligence company of copyright infringement on a massive scale. According to The Verge, the suit targets the use of tens of thousands of copyrighted musical works in training Anthropic's AI models, with the plaintiffs seeking damages of up to $150,000 per work and up to $25,000 for each instance of infringement.

Anthropric, the San Francisco-based AI company founded in 2021 by former OpenAI members Dario Amodei and Daniela Amodei, has built its reputation on Claude, a series of proprietary large language models designed to perform a wide range of tasks. Like other generative AI systems, Claude's capabilities depend on training on large datasets drawn from the internet and other sources. The question of what data can legally be used for this purpose has become increasingly contentious as the AI industry expands.

The lawsuit from Sony Music Entertainment—the recording division of Sony Group Corporation and one of the world's largest music companies—alongside Warner Chappell, one of the major music publishing entities, signals that the creative industries are prepared to pursue aggressive legal strategies against AI developers. The combination of broad infringement claims and substantial statutory damages reflects the stakes involved in this emerging area of intellectual property law.

The music industry's response to generative AI has been notably swift and forceful compared to some other sectors. Unlike some publishers or authors who have taken varied approaches to AI training, major record labels and music publishers appear unified in their view that large-scale use of their catalogs without licensing or compensation crosses a legal and ethical line. The specific damages framework—which includes both per-work penalties and per-instance charges—suggests the plaintiffs view the infringement as systematic rather than incidental.

This case arrives as Anthropic, which remains privately held, reportedly plans an initial public offering for fall 2026. A significant legal judgment or settlement could have material financial implications for the company as it prepares for public markets. More broadly, the outcome may influence how other tech companies approach data sourcing and whether existing copyright frameworks prove adequate for regulating AI training practices.
