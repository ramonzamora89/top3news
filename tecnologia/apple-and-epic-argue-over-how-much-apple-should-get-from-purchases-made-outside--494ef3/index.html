Apple and Epic argue over how much Apple should get from purchases made outside the App Store

What's happening

Apple has filed a new proposal in its ongoing legal battle with Epic Games that would allow Apple to collect fees on digital purchases made through external links outside the App Store. Epic Games has already pushed back against the proposal, setting up another round of argument over how much control and revenue Apple should retain from off-platform transactions.

Who's involved

Apple Inc., the Cupertino-based technology giant founded in 1976 by Steve Jobs and Steve Wozniak, and Epic Games, a Cary, North Carolina-based video game and software developer founded by Tim Sweeney in 1991, are the primary parties. The two companies have been engaged in a protracted legal dispute over app store policies and revenue sharing.

Why it matters

The outcome of this dispute could fundamentally reshape how app developers and platforms split revenue from digital sales and determine the extent to which Apple can monetize transactions that occur outside its controlled ecosystem. The case has implications for app store economics industry-wide and could set precedent for how much leverage platform companies can exert over developer commerce.

The story

Apple and Epic Games are locked in another critical phase of their long-running legal dispute, this time over how much revenue Apple should extract from digital purchases that bypass its App Store system entirely. According to The Verge, Apple has proposed a fee structure that would allow the company to collect payments on transactions made via external links that don't use Apple's proprietary in-app purchase system. Epic Games has already filed a response rejecting the proposal, signaling that the two companies remain far apart on fundamental questions of platform economics and control.

The dispute centers on a core tension in modern app marketplaces: whether platform owners should be able to profit from transactions that occur through alternative payment channels. Apple has long maintained that its 15 to 30 percent commission on in-app purchases is justified by the value it provides through app discovery, payment processing, and platform security. The company's latest filing suggests it wants to extend its fee collection beyond its walled garden, capturing revenue even when developers direct users to external payment methods.

Epic Games, founded in 1991 by Tim Sweeney and headquartered in North Carolina, has positioned itself as a challenger to Apple's control over app distribution and monetization. The video game and software developer built its reputation on titles like Fortnite, which generated substantial revenue through in-app purchases before becoming a flashpoint in the Apple conflict. Epic's resistance to Apple's latest proposal reflects its broader strategy of arguing that developers should have greater freedom to conduct commerce outside Apple's ecosystem without incurring additional fees.

The legal back-and-forth between these two major technology players represents a fundamental question about platform power in the digital economy. Apple, a multinational technology company headquartered in Silicon Valley since its 1976 founding, has built its business model on controlling the user experience across hardware and software. Epic's challenge to that model, now playing out in court filings and legal arguments, could reshape how the company can monetize its role as gatekeeper to hundreds of millions of devices.

The proposal and Epic's response mark another escalation in what has already become one of the most significant technology industry legal disputes in years. The case touches on issues of antitrust, fair competition, and the proper balance of power between dominant platforms and the developers who build on them. As the two companies continue to argue over fees and control, the outcome could influence how app stores operate globally and determine whether companies like Epic can sustainably challenge Apple's economic dominance.
