‘Donkey Princess’ Review: A Princess Escapes the Kingdom, but Not the Patriarchy, in an Inventive, Shape-Shifting Fairytale

What's happening

Chilean filmmaker duo Cristobal León and Joaquín Cociña have created "Donkey Princess," a new stop-motion fairytale that subverts traditional princess narratives. The film follows a princess who escapes her kingdom but discovers that freedom from her realm does not liberate her from patriarchal constraints.

Who's involved

Cristobal León and Joaquín Cociña are a Chilean stop-motion animator and filmmaker duo based in Santiago who have collaborated since 2007. They gained international recognition with their 2018 debut feature film "The Wolf House," which combined claymation, puppetry, and papier-mâché techniques to create a visually distinctive and surreal work.

Why it matters

The film represents another ambitious venture from a filmmaking duo known for pushing the boundaries of stop-motion animation and fairy tale storytelling. By centering on how escape from physical confinement doesn't necessarily dismantle deeper systemic constraints, the work engages with contemporary discussions about gender and power in a distinctly artistic context.

The story

Cristobal León and Joaquín Cociña, the Chilean filmmaking duo behind the acclaimed stop-motion feature "The Wolf House," have returned with "Donkey Princess," a new fairytale that interrogates the limits of female liberation within patriarchal systems. The film follows a princess who manages to escape the confines of her kingdom, only to discover that physical escape does not automatically free her from the patriarchal structures that define her world.

Leon and Cociña, who have been collaborating since 2007 and are based in Santiago de Chile, first gained international attention with their 2018 debut "The Wolf House." That film showcased their distinctive visual approach, layering claymation, puppetry, and papier-mâché to create a wholly original aesthetic that felt simultaneously handmade and immersively surreal. According to Variety, the duo's new work continues this inventive approach with shape-shifting elements that deepen the fairytale's thematic complexity.

The two filmmakers have built their practice around rethinking children's literature and fairytale narratives, often repositioning their visual aesthetics and storytelling conventions for contemporary audiences. Their work extends beyond animation into drawings, installations, and written texts, reflecting a multimedia approach to their artistic practice. This multidisciplinary background informs how they approach "Donkey Princess," using the fantasy genre as a vehicle for examining questions of agency and constraint.

"Donkey Princess" presents its central character's escape not as a triumphant conclusion but as the beginning of a more complex reckoning with systemic power. The film's shape-shifting visual language—a signature element of León and Cociña's methodology—mirrors this thematic focus on transformation without genuine change. Rather than offering a simple narrative of female rebellion, the work suggests that escaping a physical location requires a fundamentally different kind of escape from the ideological systems that govern one's existence.

The film's engagement with fairytale tropes reflects León and Cociña's broader artistic commitment to excavating and recontextualizing narratives that have traditionally reinforced particular social structures. By placing a princess at the center of a story that complicates rather than celebrates her liberation, they create space for audiences to examine the often-invisible architecture of patriarchal power.
