Rosie O’Donnell Should Host Her Own Show on Late Night TV — and Shatter Comedy’s Greatest Glass Ceiling

What's happening

Late-night television is in decline, with established hosts nearing the end of their tenures. A Variety opinion piece argues that Rosie O'Donnell should host her own late-night show, potentially breaking new ground in the genre.

Who's involved

Rosie O'Donnell, an American comedian and television personality who hosted the Emmy-winning syndicated daytime talk show The Rosie O'Donnell Show from 1996 to 2002. Current late-night hosts Jimmy Kimmel (ABC) and Jimmy Fallon (NBC) are also central to the discussion, as is Stephen Colbert, whose tenure at CBS's The Late Show recently concluded.

Why it matters

The proposal highlights potential opportunities to revitalize a struggling late-night format while addressing representation in a traditionally male-dominated space. A new late-night host could reshape audience expectations and bring fresh creative energy to a genre currently perceived as stagnant and aging.

The story

Late-night television faces a crisis of momentum and relevance. According to Variety, the genre is in the doldrums—not merely because hosts are taking extended summer breaks, but because the fundamental viability of the format itself appears uncertain. Both ABC's Jimmy Kimmel and NBC's Jimmy Fallon feel closer to the end of their respective tenures than the beginning, signaling potential upheaval in a programming category that has long anchored network schedules.

Into this vacuum, the argument emerges that Rosie O'Donnell should be positioned to host a late-night program. O'Donnell brings substantial credentials to such a role. After breaking through on the television series Star Search in 1984, she built a powerful media presence, ultimately hosting The Rosie O'Donnell Show, a syndicated daytime talk program that ran from 1996 to 2002 and won multiple Daytime Emmy Awards. During that run, she cultivated the nickname "Queen of Nice," establishing herself as a relatable personality capable of commanding audiences across demographics.

The late-night landscape has undergone significant transitions in recent years. Stephen Colbert, who transitioned from his acclaimed Comedy Central news satire show The Colbert Report to host CBS's The Late Show, recently concluded his tenure, opening questions about who will shape the genre's future. The proposal to bring O'Donnell into late night represents not merely a personnel decision but a potential reimagining of what late-night comedy and talk can be.

O'Donnell's track record in daytime television suggests she possesses the skills necessary to command a late-night audience. Her earlier show demonstrated an ability to balance comedy, emotional authenticity, and conversation in ways that resonated with viewers. The daytime format, while distinct from late night, required similar skills in interviewing, comedic timing, and audience connection.

The timing of this proposal reflects genuine concern about the viability of late-night television as it currently exists. Networks face pressure to attract younger audiences who increasingly consume content outside traditional broadcast schedules. A fresh voice and perspective in the late-night slot could potentially reverse declining viewership and reignite interest in a format that has weathered technological disruption and changing media consumption habits for decades.
