Liam and Noel Gallagher Raise Fists and Electrify Venice as Oasis Doc Gets 8-Minute Standing Ovation

What's happening

Liam and Noel Gallagher presented their documentary "Don't Look Back in Anger" at the Venice Film Festival, where the film received an eight-minute standing ovation. The estranged brothers appeared together on stage, holding hands as the world premiere unfolded before an enthusiastic crowd.

Who's involved

Liam and Noel Gallagher are the founding members of Oasis, the English rock band formed in Manchester in 1991 that became one of the most globally successful groups of the Britpop era. The Venice Film Festival is the world's oldest film festival and one of the five most prestigious international film festivals worldwide.

Why it matters

The public reconciliation between the Gallagher brothers marks a significant moment for the band and their fanbase, as the siblings famously split Oasis in 2009 amid internal conflict. The documentary's premiere at such a prestigious venue signals renewed interest in the band's legacy and the broader cultural impact of their story.

The story

Oasis mania descended on the Venice Film Festival on Saturday night as Liam and Noel Gallagher presented the world premiere of their highly anticipated documentary "Don't Look Back in Anger." According to Variety, the rock film earned an eight-minute standing ovation as the once-estranged brothers held hands and raised their fists to acknowledge the crowd's enthusiasm.

Oasis formed in Manchester in 1991, with Liam initially recruiting his older brother Noel as a fifth member to solidify the group's lineup. Noel became the de facto leader and primary songwriter for the band's first four albums. The group rose to prominence during the Britpop movement and became one of the most globally successful rock bands of their era, defining the sound and attitude of 1990s British popular culture.

The brothers' relationship fractured publicly over the years, culminating in the band's breakup in 2009. Since then, Liam and Noel pursued separate musical careers and maintained a contentious public relationship marked by occasional verbal sparring and conflicting statements about the possibility of a reunion. Their appearance together at Venice, holding hands on stage as their documentary premiered, represents a notable moment of reconciliation that will resonate deeply with the band's devoted fanbase worldwide.

The Venice Film Festival, held annually in Venice, is the world's oldest film festival and ranks among the "Big Five" international film festivals globally. The festival's prestige and reach make it an ideal venue for the Oasis documentary's debut, ensuring significant media attention and cultural resonance for the project.

The eight-minute standing ovation underscores the enduring cultural significance of Oasis and the appetite for their story. The documentary's premiere comes at a time when interest in 1990s British rock culture has resurged, suggesting potential broader appeal for the film beyond longtime fans of the band.
