‘Boorman and the Devil’ Review: The Making of John Boorman’s 1977 Disaster ‘Exorcist II: The Heretic’ Becomes a Documentary So Unbelievable It’s Funny

What's happening

A new documentary titled 'Boorman and the Devil' examines the chaotic production of John Boorman's infamous 1977 film 'Exorcist II: The Heretic,' revealing the director's delusional creative ambitions that resulted in unintentional comedy.

Who's involved

John Boorman is a acclaimed British filmmaker known for classics like 'Deliverance' (1972), 'Zardoz' (1974), and 'Excalibur' (1981). The documentary focuses on his ill-fated sequel to William Goodhart's 1973 horror hit 'The Exorcist,' which starred Linda Blair and Richard Burton.

Why it matters

The documentary reveals how even established auteurs can produce critically panned films when ambition outpaces execution, offering insight into Hollywood's creative risks and failures. For film historians and fans of cinema history, it documents a fascinating case study in directorial hubris and the gap between artistic vision and audience reception.

The story

A new documentary is shedding light on one of cinema's most notorious production disasters: John Boorman's 1977 sequel 'Exorcist II: The Heretic.' The film, which starred Linda Blair, Richard Burton, and Louise Fletcher, became a punchline in horror history, but according to Variety, the story of its making is so absurd it reads like a Christopher Guest mockumentary brought to life.

Boorman, a filmmaker with an impressive track record including 'Deliverance' and the visionary sci-fi oddity 'Zardoz,' approached the sequel with what can only be described as unhinged ambition. Rather than deliver a straightforward follow-up to the 1973 original, Boorman attempted to transform the horror franchise into something entirely different—a high-minded supernatural science fiction film that bore little resemblance to what audiences expected or wanted. His fortitude in pursuing this vision might be admirable in abstract terms, but 'Boorman and the Devil' documents the results as something closer to delusional filmmaking.

The documentary captures the dark comedy of watching a respected director become consumed by his own grandiose vision. Set four years after the events of the original film, 'Exorcist II' attempted to expand the mythology in ways that contemporary audiences found baffling rather than terrifying. The film's notorious reputation has only grown with time, becoming a cultural reference point for ambitious sequels that fundamentally misunderstand their source material.

What makes the documentary compelling is how it traces the gap between intention and execution. Boorman's commitment to his vision remained unwavering even as the production descended into chaos and the finished film faced near-universal dismissal. The documentary presents this not as a tale of noble failure, but as a cautionary study in how utterly unhinged creative hubris can produce something inadvertently hilarious.

For film historians, 'Exorcist II: The Heretic' has long been fertile ground for analysis—a case where a major studio greenlit and released a major star vehicle that audiences and critics rejected almost universally. Boorman would go on to direct 'Excalibur' in 1981, proving his commercial recovery was possible, but the shadow of the 1977 sequel has never entirely lifted from his filmography.
