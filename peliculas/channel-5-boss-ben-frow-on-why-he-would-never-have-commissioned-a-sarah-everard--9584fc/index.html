Channel 5 Boss Ben Frow on Why He Would Never Have Commissioned a Sarah Everard Drama: ‘There Are Some Things I Just Wouldn’t Do’

What's happening

Channel 5 chief content officer Ben Frow has stated he would never commission a drama about Sarah Everard's murder, even as the BBC prepares to air a scripted investigation into the case written by acclaimed screenwriter Jeff Pope.

Who's involved

Ben Frow is the chief content officer at Channel 5, a Paramount-owned public service broadcaster. Jeff Pope is a BAFTA-winning television producer and screenwriter known for factual dramas including See No Evil: The Moors Murders.

Why it matters

Frow's comments highlight ongoing sensitivity around dramatizing real-life tragedies involving victims of violent crime, particularly high-profile cases that generated significant public attention and trauma. His stance reveals internal editorial boundaries at major networks regarding which true crimes are considered appropriate for dramatization, even as other broadcasters greenlight similar projects.

The story

Channel 5 boss Ben Frow has drawn a clear line on what stories his network will not tell, declaring he would never have commissioned a drama centered on Sarah Everard's murder. The remarks come as the BBC moves forward with a scripted investigation into the case, penned by acclaimed screenwriter Jeff Pope, according to Variety.

Frow's position underscores a divide within the British broadcasting industry over the dramatization of real-world tragedies. While the BBC has deemed Everard's case appropriate subject matter for a drama series—with Pope, a veteran of factual storytelling, handling the script—Channel 5's leadership has determined such a project would cross an editorial line. Frow stated, "There are some things I just wouldn't do," signaling a principled stance on the boundaries of entertainment drawn from genuine suffering.

Sarah Everard, 33, was kidnapped on the evening of March 3, 2021, while walking home to Brixton Hill after visiting a friend near Clapham Common in South London. She was stopped by Wayne Couzens, an off-duty Metropolitan Police constable who identified himself as a police officer and handcuffed her. Couzens drove Everard to Kent, where he raped and strangled her, then burned her body and disposed of her remains in a pond. The case became a flashpoint for national conversations about violence against women and the accountability of police officers, given the perpetrator's position of authority.

Pope, who won a BAFTA in 2006 for the drama See No Evil: The Moors Murders, brings substantial credentials in transforming historical crimes into television narratives. His track record suggests the BBC drama will approach the Everard case with the gravity and craftsmanship expected of serious factual programming. However, Frow's comments suggest that not all networks share the judgment that such a project serves the public interest, or that the emotional rawness of the case remains too immediate for dramatization.

The question of when and how to adapt real tragedies for drama remains contested in British television. Frow's refusal to commission such a project reflects broader editorial caution at some broadcasters, even as others proceed, indicating that decisions about which crimes deserve dramatization are neither universal nor settled.
