Owner of ‘Heated Rivalry’ Cabin Needed to Do Some ‘Soul Searching’ Before Inviting the Show Back for Season 2

What's happening

Michael Kutryk, owner of the cabin that served as a filming location for HBO's "Heated Rivalry" Season 1, is reconsidering whether to allow the production to return for Season 2. Kutryk says he needed to undergo "soul searching" before making a decision about hosting the show again.

Who's involved

Michael Kutryk is the private property owner whose cabin became notable as a set location for the HBO series. "Heated Rivalry" is a Canadian sports romance series created by Jacob Tierney for Crave, based on Rachel Reid's Game Changers book series, starring Hudson Williams and Connor Storrie as rival professional hockey players in a secret relationship.

Why it matters

The hesitation signals potential behind-the-scenes friction between production and location owners, which could affect Season 2 filming logistics and timeline. It also highlights the practical challenges of producing prestige television when private residences become central to a show's identity and public recognition.

The story

Michael Kutryk, the owner of a cabin that gained prominence as a key filming location for "Heated Rivalry" Season 1, has indicated he is weighing whether to permit HBO's production to use his property again for the show's second season. According to Variety, Kutryk stated that he "did a little bit of soul searching about allowing them to come back," suggesting some uncertainty about a return engagement.

"Heated Rivalry" is a Canadian sports romance series created, written, and directed by Jacob Tierney for Crave. The show, based on Rachel Reid's Game Changers book series and taking its title from the 2019 second installment, follows Shane Hollander and Ilya Rozanov, two professional hockey players who maintain a secret long-term romantic relationship while competing for rival teams. The series has generated significant audience interest, making its filming locations—including Kutryk's cabin—subjects of public curiosity.

The specifics of Kutryk's reservations remain unclear from his initial remarks. His reference to "soul searching" suggests a period of personal deliberation about the disruption, demands, or other aspects of hosting a major production on his property. For television productions, securing consistent access to distinctive private locations is crucial to maintaining visual continuity and the specific aesthetic that audiences associate with a show.

The situation underscores a recurring tension in television production: the need for filmmakers to secure appealing, authentic locations versus the legitimate concerns of property owners about privacy, property wear, and the intrusion of commercial entertainment activity into their lives. Kutryk's hesitation may reflect practical considerations—from construction wear to the loss of privacy—or other factors related to his experience hosting the first season.

With Season 2 presumably in development or pre-production planning stages, producers will need to resolve this question relatively soon. If Kutryk ultimately declines to host the production again, HBO and Tierney's team would face the challenge of finding an alternative location that matches the cabin's established visual identity, or redesigning set elements to accommodate a new space.
